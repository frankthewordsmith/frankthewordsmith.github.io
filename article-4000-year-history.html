A 4,000-Year History of Usury

What changes when the history of lending is reconstructed from discrete primary-source records rather than from a single continuous story?

Start with the evidence

A long history of lending cannot be reduced to one uninterrupted definition of “usury.” The surviving evidence belongs to different legal, economic and religious settings, and the sources themselves differ in what they preserve. The research archive therefore treats each major claim as a source record and separates surviving primary text from reconstruction, later interpretation and modern scholarship.

The earliest records in the archive already show that interest-bearing loans were a recognizable institution. The Laws of Ur-Nammu preserve provisions concerning barley and silver loans. The relevant text is fragmentary and survives as a scholarly composite: CDLI records the barley provision at lines d036–d038 and the silver provision at d039–d041. The archive consequently grades this evidence as reconstructed/composite rather than presenting it as one intact tablet.

Before Hammurabi

The Laws of Eshnunna provide another early legal witness. Law 18a specifies calculations for silver and grain loans, conventionally expressed as 20% for silver and approximately 33% for grain. These figures are rates specified by a legal provision; they should not automatically be treated as universal market rates for every transaction.

These Mesopotamian records matter because they show that lending, debt and interest were already objects of legal attention before the famous Code of Hammurabi. They do not, however, establish that all lending followed one standardized practice.

Hammurabi: regulation, not a modern banking code

The Code of Hammurabi contains provisions on debt, lending, agricultural risk and interest. Section 48 is especially important: when crop failure affects the debtor, the text provides relief concerning interest for the affected year. This is secure evidence for legal attention to debt risk.

The familiar numerical rates of 20% for silver and about 33⅓% for barley require more caution. The corresponding portion of the surviving stele is damaged, and the research record identifies those rate provisions as reconstructed from other textual witnesses. The article therefore does not present the rates as though the entire passage survives intact on the famous stele.

The recurring historical question

Across later periods, the historical question changes form: who may lend, what counts as an excessive return, whether hardship changes the obligation, and whether religious or political authorities should regulate the transaction. Roman law, Christian writers, Islamic scripture and jurisprudence, Jewish legal writing, medieval scholasticism and modern legislation answer those questions within their own institutions.

The archive's method is deliberately cumulative but not flattening. A Roman legal provision is not evidence of a later Christian rule merely because both concern interest. A Qur’anic verse is not identical to a later juristic category. A nineteenth-century repeal statute does not retroactively describe ancient lending practice.

From prohibition to regulation

Modern financial systems eventually developed extensive statutory regimes for banking, disclosure, monetary policy and consumer protection. The research timeline includes major transitions such as the Bank of England's 1694 charter, the nineteenth-century repeal of usury restrictions, the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, the Banking Acts of 1933 and 1935, Bretton Woods, the end of dollar convertibility in 1971, the Jamaica arrangements of 1976 and Dodd–Frank in 2010.

These events should be read as institutional milestones, not as proof of a single linear progression from “primitive” lending to modern banking. Different rules address different problems, and the sources preserve different kinds of evidence.

What the 4,000-year record can actually show

The strongest conclusion is methodological: recognizable forms of lending and interest are very old, while the legal and moral treatment of them has repeatedly changed. The historical record supports a chronology of changing institutions and arguments, not a single timeless definition of usury.

For the detailed evidence, use the timeline, research library and bibliography. Individual claims should be checked against their source records rather than against this synthesis alone.
