From Jackson’s Bank War to Lincoln’s National Banking System: The Transformation of American Money and Banking, 1816–1864

A source-conscious chronology of the institutional transformation between the Second Bank of the United States, Jackson’s Bank War, the state/free-banking era, Civil War finance, greenbacks and the National Banking Acts.

Introduction: a thirty-year institutional transformation

The history from Andrew Jackson’s destruction of the Second Bank of the United States to Abraham Lincoln’s wartime monetary and banking reforms is not a simple story of one president destroying a bank and another replacing it. Between 1816 and 1864 the United States passed through several distinct financial arrangements: a federally chartered national bank, decentralized state banking, experiments with an Independent Treasury, repeated financial crises, Civil War borrowing, federal paper money, and finally a nationally chartered banking system.

The central question is therefore institutional: how did the United States move from a national banking structure to decentralized banking and then back toward federal control of currency and banking?

1. 1816 — The Second Bank of the United States

The Second Bank was chartered in 1816 after the War of 1812 exposed serious weaknesses in the fragmented American banking system. The First Bank’s charter had expired in 1811, and wartime financing difficulties, bank-note disorder and widespread suspension of specie payments strengthened the case for another national institution.

The Second Bank received a twenty-year federal charter, $35 million in capital, a Philadelphia headquarters and branches around the country. It held federal deposits and served as the government’s fiscal agent. It also influenced state banks by presenting their notes for redemption and demanding specie. It was not a modern Federal Reserve-style central bank, but it performed several functions later associated with central banking.

2. 1819 — Panic and the Bank’s troubled beginning

The Second Bank’s early management was controversial. Under William Jones it expanded credit and then contracted sharply. The financial environment surrounding the Panic of 1819 damaged the Bank’s reputation and led to tighter management under Langdon Cheves.

Nicholas Biddle became president in 1823 and strengthened the institution. The Bank became more effective at disciplining state-bank note issuance, transferring funds and integrating a geographically fragmented financial system. Its effectiveness, however, also made its concentration of power more visible.

3. 1828–1832 — Andrew Jackson and the Bank War

Andrew Jackson entered the presidency in 1829 already suspicious of banks, paper money and concentrated financial power. His opposition combined constitutional, political and economic arguments. He regarded the Bank as privileged, too powerful and insufficiently accountable to ordinary citizens and the states.

In 1832 Nicholas Biddle and his allies sought an early recharter. Congress passed the bill, but Jackson vetoed it on July 10, 1832. His veto message is the principal primary source for his constitutional and economic case. Jackson then won reelection and treated the result as political support for his opposition to the Bank.

4. 1833–1836 — Federal deposits are removed and the Bank loses its charter

Jackson next ordered federal deposits removed from the Second Bank and placed in selected state-chartered banks, later called “pet banks.” Treasury Secretary William J. Duane resisted and was removed. Roger B. Taney, serving as acting Treasury secretary, carried out the removal in September 1833.

The Senate censured Jackson in March 1834, making the dispute a constitutional struggle as well as a banking dispute. The federal charter expired in 1836. The institution continued under a Pennsylvania charter as the United States Bank of Pennsylvania and eventually failed in the early 1840s.

The precise historical formulation is therefore: Jackson destroyed the Second Bank as a federally chartered national institution. He did not simply close the institution in 1833.

5. 1837 and after — The decentralized banking era

After the Bank War, the United States did not create another national central bank. State-chartered banks proliferated and issued their own notes. A note issued by one bank could trade at a discount elsewhere because its acceptability depended on the issuing bank and local conditions.

The Panic of 1837 occurred in this new institutional environment. It is not historically sufficient to say that Jackson alone caused the Panic. Explanations involve banking and credit contraction, land and commodity speculation, specie movements and international financial conditions. The Bank War nevertheless mattered because the crisis occurred after the country had lost its national banking institution.

6. The Specie Circular and the hard-money debate

Jackson’s Specie Circular of 1836 required gold or silver for federal public-land purchases. It reflected his preference for specie and his opposition to speculative expansion based on bank credit.

The larger question was fundamental: should the federal government rely on privately issued bank notes, or should public finance be based more directly on specie? The answer remained unsettled after Jackson left office.

7. 1840–1846 — The Independent Treasury experiments

Without a national bank, another problem remained: where should the federal government keep its money? The Independent Treasury system separated federal funds from commercial banks. It was enacted in 1840, modified after political changes, and restored in 1846.

The restored system created subtreasuries through which the federal government held and transferred its own funds. This was another institutional response to the question of how government finance should relate to commercial banking.

8. 1841 — Another national-bank proposal fails

Whig leaders attempted to establish another national bank in 1841. President John Tyler vetoed the legislation. The United States therefore remained without a federally chartered national bank.

This failure is important because it demonstrates that the absence of a national bank was not simply an accidental gap. It was repeatedly reinforced by constitutional disagreement and political conflict.

9. 1846–1850s — Expansion without a unified currency

The Mexican-American War increased federal borrowing and spending. During the following decade the American economy expanded rapidly, with railroads, western settlement, land speculation and commercial activity growing together.

Yet the banking structure remained fragmented. The national economy was becoming increasingly integrated while the paper-currency system remained heavily dependent on state-chartered banks and their individual notes.

10. 1857 — Another major financial crisis

The Panic of 1857 exposed continuing weaknesses in the decentralized system. The failure of the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company helped trigger the crisis, which spread through financial markets.

As with the Panic of 1837, the causes were multiple. Speculation, banking conditions, railroad finance and international developments all mattered. The institutional fact is that the United States still lacked a central bank capable of acting as a modern lender of last resort.

11. 1860–1861 — Lincoln and the Civil War financing crisis

Abraham Lincoln was elected in November 1860. Secession and the Civil War then transformed the monetary question into an emergency. The federal government needed unprecedented amounts of money for soldiers, equipment, transportation, food, ships and other wartime expenditures, while disruption of Southern trade reduced customs revenue.

Salmon P. Chase, Lincoln’s Treasury Secretary, initially relied heavily on borrowing and bond finance. The Treasury negotiated with banking institutions in New York, Boston and Philadelphia. A major 1861 arrangement involved $150 million of advances in three $50 million installments through three-year Treasury securities paying 7.30 percent interest, the basis of the famous Seven-Thirties financing.

12. The Rothschild question

There was also a documented channel between the Union’s financing efforts and the Rothschild banking network. August Belmont, the American representative of the Rothschild interests, was involved in efforts to explore European financing for the Union in 1861. Correspondence between Belmont and Lionel de Rothschild survives in the Abraham Lincoln Papers.

The evidence should be stated precisely. It supports an attempt to obtain Rothschild participation in Union financing; it does not establish that the Rothschild family made a specific direct loan to Lincoln that Lincoln rejected. The major documented 1861 domestic financing was arranged with banks in New York, Boston and Philadelphia.

13. 1862 — Greenbacks

Borrowing alone did not solve the Union’s financing and currency problems. The United States still had a fragmented paper-money system dominated by state-bank notes. The federal government therefore moved toward issuing its own paper currency.

The Legal Tender Act of 1862 authorized $150 million of United States Notes, later called greenbacks. They were federal government-issued, non-interest-bearing notes that were legal tender and were not directly convertible into gold or silver.

Salmon P. Chase had reservations about an unlimited paper currency because of the risk of inflation. The measure nevertheless became necessary in the wartime emergency. Additional issues were authorized, eventually bringing the amount authorized during the war to roughly $400 million.

14. Greenbacks did not solve the banking problem

Greenbacks addressed one problem: the federal government now had a nationally issued paper currency. But state banks continued to issue their own notes. The country therefore still had federal notes, state-bank notes, coins, deposits and government securities operating within a fragmented banking structure.

Chase and Congress therefore pursued a second reform: a federally regulated national banking system.

15. 1863 — The National Currency Act

The National Currency Act of February 25, 1863 established the framework for federally chartered national banks and created the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. National banks could issue standardized notes backed by United States government securities.

This linked three things: federal debt, national banks and a uniform bank-note system. The objective was not simply to recreate the Second Bank. National banks were privately owned and federally chartered and supervised, rather than being one powerful government-linked corporation.

16. 1864 — The National Banking Act

The 1864 legislation strengthened the national banking framework. National banks operated under federal supervision and issued notes secured by U.S. Treasury securities deposited with the government. By the end of 1864 hundreds of banks had received national charters.

The architecture was fundamentally different from the Second Bank: there was no single national banking corporation with a nationwide branch network. Instead, the federal government created a regulated network of privately owned national banks and a standardized national bank-note system.

17. 1865 — The state-bank-note tax

The transformation became more decisive in 1865, when Congress imposed a 10 percent tax on state-bank notes. The measure sharply reduced their circulation and accelerated the shift toward national bank notes.

Thus the Civil War-era reforms moved the United States from a fragmented system of state-issued bank notes toward a federally regulated national currency structure.

19. Greenbacks and national bank notes were not the same

This distinction is essential. A greenback was a United States Note issued by the federal government. A national bank note was issued by a privately owned federally chartered bank and backed by U.S. government securities under the national banking system.

The two forms of paper money therefore represented different institutional relationships between the government and the banking system.

20. The larger historical chain

The institutional sequence is:

First Bank expires (1811) → War of 1812 exposes weaknesses → Second Bank created (1816) → Panic of 1819 → Biddle strengthens the Bank → Jackson attacks it → recharter veto (1832) → federal deposits removed (1833) → charter expires (1836) → state/free banking → Panic of 1837 → Independent Treasury experiments → Panic of 1857 → Lincoln elected (1860) → Civil War financing crisis → greenbacks (1862) → National Currency Act (1863) → National Banking Act (1864) → state-bank-note tax (1865).

21. Why the story continues to 1913

The National Banking Acts solved important problems of currency uniformity and federal banking supervision, but they did not create a modern central bank. Later panics—including those of 1873, 1884, 1893 and 1907—demonstrated continuing weaknesses in reserve mobility and crisis management.

The Panic of 1907 generated renewed pressure for central-banking reform. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 eventually created the Federal Reserve System.

The long chain can therefore be summarized as:

First Bank → Second Bank → Jackson’s Bank War → decentralized state banking → recurring financial crises → Civil War → greenbacks → National Banking System → further crises → Federal Reserve.

22. Causation: what should not be oversimplified

Jackson should not be described as the sole cause of every later banking crisis. Nor should greenbacks be described simply as a response to high-interest loans from private bankers. The evidence points to interacting causes: wartime expenditure, disrupted customs revenue, borrowing constraints, fragmented state-bank currency, the absence of a national banking institution and the Treasury’s search for a more unified financial structure.

Likewise, the Rothschild connection should be kept separate from the documented domestic bank financing. A financing approach through Belmont and the Rothschild network is not the same thing as a completed Rothschild loan.

23. Primary sources and evidentiary hierarchy

Primary evidence: Andrew Jackson’s July 10, 1832 Bank veto; Jackson’s messages concerning federal deposits; McCulloch v. Maryland (1819); congressional debates; Senate censure proceedings; Treasury reports; the Legal Tender Act; the National Currency Act; the National Banking Act; Civil War financing documents; and surviving correspondence involving August Belmont and the Rothschild banking house.

Institutional research: Federal Reserve History, U.S. Treasury, U.S. Senate, Library of Congress, OCC and FRASER archival material.

Academic research: economic and financial histories of the Second Bank, Jacksonian monetary policy, the Panics of 1819, 1837 and 1857, Civil War finance, greenbacks and the National Banking Acts.

The methodological rule is to keep documented statements, later interpretation and causal reconstruction visibly separate.

24. Central research question

How did the United States move from Andrew Jackson’s destruction of the Second Bank of the United States to Abraham Lincoln’s wartime creation of federal paper money and a nationally chartered banking system—and what major financial, political, constitutional and economic events transformed that system in the intervening decades?

The answer is not a single presidential decision. It is a sequence of institutional experiments in banking, public finance, currency and federal authority stretching from the Second Bank’s creation in 1816 through the National Banking Acts of 1863–64 and beyond.
