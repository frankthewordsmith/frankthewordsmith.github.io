What Hammurabi Actually Regulated

A close look at debt, agricultural failure and interest in the Code of Hammurabi—and at the difference between surviving text and reconstruction.

The first question is what survives

The Code of Hammurabi is often invoked as though every famous provision survives in an equally secure form. It does not. For research purposes, the distinction between surviving sections and reconstructed passages is essential.

The archive's Hammurabi record treats §§48–52 as the secure cluster relevant to debt and agricultural conditions. It separately identifies the famous interest-rate provisions as belonging to a damaged portion of the stele and therefore as reconstructed from other witnesses.

Section 48 and agricultural risk

Section 48 connects debt obligations with agricultural failure. In the research record, its significance is that the legal text addresses the consequences of crop failure rather than treating the debtor's obligation as completely detached from production conditions.

This is important because it changes the historical question. The evidence is not simply “Hammurabi set an interest rate.” It shows a legal framework in which debt, interest and agricultural risk could be related. The provision should still be read on its own terms rather than projected into a modern concept such as bankruptcy protection.

What about 20% and 33⅓%?

Standard scholarship associates Old Babylonian lending with rates commonly expressed as 20% for silver and approximately 33⅓% for barley. Those figures are historically important, but the archive deliberately labels the Hammurabi rate passage as reconstructed because the relevant portion of the stele is damaged.

That qualification matters. A reconstructed rate can be historically useful without being described as an intact quotation from the surviving stele. The research record therefore points readers to Martha T. Roth's Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor and distinguishes the secure debt provisions from the reconstructed rate passage.

Law is not the same thing as every transaction

The Code should also not be treated as a complete statistical record of Babylonian lending. A law collection is normative evidence. Loan contracts and other documentary sources provide a different window into actual transactions and can show how particular arrangements worked in practice.

That distinction prevents a common historical shortcut: taking a legally specified or customary rate and assuming that every loan in every circumstance carried exactly that rate.

Why this matters

Hammurabi's significance lies in the evidence for legal regulation of debt, lending and agricultural risk. The strongest source-conscious reading does not require the famous numerical rates to do more work than the surviving evidence permits.
