The Chronological Development of Ribā in the Qur’an

From Moral Warning to Final Prohibition: a source-conscious reconstruction of the Qur’anic treatment of ribā.

Introduction: A Prohibition Revealed in Stages

The Qur’anic treatment of ribā is distributed across four principal passages: 30:39, 3:130, 4:161, and 2:275–282. Read through the traditional chronology of revelation, these passages present a development from an early moral conception of increase to an explicit prohibition and, finally, to a wider framework governing debt, principal, creditor conduct, debtor hardship, documentation, and accountability before Allah.

The development should not, however, be reduced to a mechanically “four-stage” prohibition. The earliest passage, 30:39, is interpreted differently by early exegetes from the debt-based ribā described in the later Medinan passages. Likewise, 4:161 refers to an earlier prohibition imposed upon the Jews rather than introducing a new Muslim legal rule.

The most defensible reconstruction is therefore one of progressive specification: moral diagnosis, identification of a concrete debt practice, continuity with earlier revelation, comprehensive prohibition, enforcement, protection of the distressed debtor, and regulation of legitimate debt.

1. Qur’an 30:39 — The First Appearance of Ribā

In Sūrat al-Rūm, the Qur’an says:

“Whatever you give as ribā so that it may increase through the wealth of people will not increase with Allah. But whatever you give in zakāh, seeking the pleasure of Allah—those are the ones who will receive manifold increase.”
— Qur’an 30:39

This is the earliest Qur’anic occurrence of the term ribā in the traditional chronology. The verse contrasts an increase sought through another person’s wealth with wealth given for Allah’s sake. It does not yet use the later formula, “Allah has forbidden ribā”; its emphasis is moral and theological: an apparent increase in wealth is not necessarily an increase recognized by Allah.

This distinction matters because early tafsir does not speak with one voice about the precise economic referent of 30:39. Al-Ṭabarī records interpretations in which the verse concerns a gift or payment made with the expectation of receiving something greater in return. That evidence cautions against simply imposing the fully developed later legal definition of ribā upon the earliest occurrence.

2. Qur’an 3:130 — The Known Debt-Increase Practice

The language becomes direct in Sūrat Āl ʿImrān:

“O believers! Do not consume ribā, multiplied many times over. And be mindful of Allah so that you may prosper.”
— Qur’an 3:130

Here the address is explicitly to believers and the language is prohibitive. Early exegetical reports describe the practice behind “doubled and multiplied” as a debt that increased when the debtor could not pay at maturity and received an extension of time in exchange for a larger obligation.

Al-Ṭabarī preserves reports describing the mechanism in essentially these terms: a debt reaches its due date; the debtor cannot pay; the term is extended; and the amount owed is increased. The increase can then recur when the extended term expires. citeturn0search3

The phrase “doubled and multiplied” should therefore be read as a description of the known practice, not as a numerical threshold implying that a smaller increase would automatically be lawful. The later passage in 2:275–279 gives the broader formulation and distinguishes ribā from permitted trade while preserving the creditor’s principal. Classical commentary likewise treats the wording of 3:130 as descriptive of the prevailing practice rather than a safe-rate exception. citeturn0search10

3. Qur’an 4:161 — Continuity with Earlier Revelation

“And for taking ribā, although they had been forbidden from it, and for consuming people’s wealth unjustly…”
— Qur’an 4:161

Qur’an 4:161 has a different function. The subject is an earlier community, and the verse explicitly says that ribā had already been forbidden to them. It is therefore not a new prohibition addressed to Muslims.

The verse places ribā within a longer history of divine law. In the classical interpretation represented by al-Ṭabarī, the practice is associated with taking an excess over principal in connection with postponing repayment. The Qur’anic presentation thus moves from the moral concept of increase toward a recognizable creditor–debtor mechanism, while also presenting the prohibition as part of an earlier prophetic tradition.

4. Qur’an 2:275–277 — The Comprehensive Legal Framework

The final major Qur’anic treatment appears in Sūrat al-Baqarah:

“Allah has permitted trade and forbidden ribā.”
— Qur’an 2:275

This is the clearest general legal formulation. The Qur’an does not prohibit economic gain as such: trade remains permitted. The distinction is between lawful trade and ribā.

Verses 276–277 return to the moral language introduced in 30:39. Ribā is contrasted with charity, and the believer’s economic conduct is placed alongside faith, righteous action, prayer, and zakāh. The final passage therefore does more than state a prohibition; it places economic behavior within a wider moral order.

Read chronologically, the connection is notable: the early question of whether an apparent increase truly “increases with Allah” is taken up again in the final passage, where ribā is set against charity and the believer’s broader obligations.

5. Qur’an 2:278–280 — From Prohibition to Enforcement and Mercy

The next verses move from general prohibition to existing claims:

“O believers! Fear Allah and give up whatever remains of ribā, if you are truly believers.”
— Qur’an 2:278

The command is no longer simply to avoid entering into ribā. It addresses what remains outstanding. Classical reports connect this passage with a dispute involving Thaqīf and Banū al-Mughīrah after the conquest of Makkah. The historical reports are important because they show the prohibition being applied to existing claims rather than treated only as a rule for future contracts.

Verse 279 then states the principle with unusual precision:

“But if you repent, then you may have your principal. You will neither wrong nor be wronged.”
— Qur’an 2:279

The creditor retains the principal, but not the ribā increase. The final phrase places both sides within a principle of mutual non-injustice: the creditor is not entitled to the excess, while the debtor is not entitled to withhold the principal.

Verse 280 immediately addresses hardship:

“If the debtor is in hardship, then grant him respite until ease. And if you waive it as charity, that is better for you, if only you knew.”
— Qur’an 2:280

This is a crucial part of the chronology. The final treatment does not merely remove an increase from the debt relationship; it also replaces escalation against a distressed debtor with respite and commendation of forgiveness.

6. Qur’an 2:281–282 — Accountability and Legitimate Debt

Verse 281 turns from the immediate economic relationship to final accountability:

“And fear a Day when you will be returned to Allah.”
— Qur’an 2:281

Reports attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās identify this verse as the last verse revealed, while another well-known report identifies the “verse of ribā” as the last revelation. Classical scholars, including Ibn Ḥajar, reconciled these reports by treating 2:281 as the concluding verse of the final ribā passage. This remains a strong classical position, but it should not be presented as an uncontested historical fact.

There is competing evidence. A report from al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim identifies the verse concerning kalālah (4:176) as the last verse revealed. The responsible historical formulation is therefore to distinguish the strongest classical identification from absolute certainty about the final individual verse.

Immediately after 2:281 comes 2:282, the famous Debt Verse, which instructs believers to document a deferred debt in writing and use witnesses. This is significant for the chronology: the Qur’an does not abolish debt. It prohibits ribā while retaining legitimate debt and regulating how such obligations should be documented.

8. What the Chronology Shows

The development is more precise than the simple phrase “gradual prohibition of interest” suggests. The Qur’anic treatment moves through several connected questions:

  • Moral diagnosis: what appears to be an increase in wealth may not constitute genuine increase with Allah.
  • Identification: a known debt practice involving an increased obligation in exchange for delay is explicitly condemned.
  • Continuity: ribā is presented as something already prohibited in an earlier divine tradition.
  • General rule: trade is permitted while ribā is forbidden.
  • Enforcement: outstanding ribā claims must be abandoned, while principal remains legitimate.
  • Debtor protection: hardship calls for respite rather than further escalation.
  • Accountability: economic conduct is placed under ultimate judgment before Allah.
  • Legitimate debt: debt itself remains permissible and is subject to documentation and witnessing.

The result is therefore not simply a prohibition of one financial technique. It is a restructuring of the creditor–debtor relationship around distinctions between trade and ribā, principal and excess, legitimate debt and exploitative increase, creditor rights and debtor protection.

9. The Final Historical Caution

The broad chronology is stronger than any attempt to reconstruct an exact verse-by-verse timetable. We can place 30:39 in the Makkan period; 3:130 and 4:161 in the Medinan period; and 2:275–282 in the late Medinan phase. Classical reports also connect 2:278–280 with the practical settlement of outstanding pre-Islamic ribā claims.

What the evidence does not establish is a precise day-by-day sequence for 2:278, 2:279, 2:280, 2:281 and 2:282. Traditional sūrah chronology generally establishes the period of a sūrah’s revelation rather than providing an exact chronology for every individual verse.

Conclusion: From “Increase” to a Complete Ethics of Debt

The Qur’anic treatment of ribā develops from an early moral conception of increase into an explicit prohibition and finally into a comprehensive framework for debt.

30:39 asks what kind of increase genuinely counts before Allah. 3:130 identifies and prohibits a concrete debt-escalation practice. 4:161 places the issue within a history of earlier divine prohibition. 2:275–282 then supplies the comprehensive framework: trade is permitted, ribā is forbidden, outstanding ribā must be abandoned, principal remains, distressed debtors receive respite, economic conduct is subject to final accountability, and legitimate debt is documented.

The historical evidence therefore supports a careful conclusion: the Qur’anic treatment of ribā developed from moral critique to explicit prohibition and finally to a broader restructuring of the creditor–debtor relationship. The final stage belongs to the very last phase of Qur’anic revelation, while the exact chronology of the individual closing verses remains subject to the limitations of the surviving reports.
