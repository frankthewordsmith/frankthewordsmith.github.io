Indian Railway Gold-Interest and Exchange Burden, 1891

Date: 4 August 1891

Evidence grade: A

Primary source

House of Commons, Hansard, 4 August 1891, vol. 356, cols. 1324–1325, East India (Revenue Accounts).

Official Hansard record

Exact primary passage

“most of the money with which these railways have been constructed has been borrowed in England, the interest has to be paid in gold; and, owing to the fall in the rate of exchange, the profit of 4¾ per cent., which would be otherwise realised by Indian railways, is turned into a loss, and every year the revenues of India are charged with a certain sum in respect of the excess of railway expenditure over railway receipts.”

What the passage establishes

The official parliamentary statement links Indian railway finance to English borrowing, gold-denominated interest payments, exchange-rate movements, and a resulting charge against Indian revenues when the exchange movement converted an otherwise positive railway return into a loss.

Evidence limits

The passage does not quantify the full lifetime exchange burden, identify every borrowing instrument or lender, or establish that every railway line was financed in the same manner. It records the stated mechanism and its effect on Indian revenues.

Historical significance

This is a distinct sovereign-finance mechanism: external borrowing currency and the currency of interest payment created an exchange exposure capable of transferring railway-finance losses to Indian revenues.
