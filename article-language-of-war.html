Why the Language of War?

Qur’an 2:275–279 contains some of the strongest language in the Qur’anic discussion of ribā. What does the passage itself say?

Begin with the passage

The research record identifies Qur’an 2:275–279 as a primary-text record with a stable verse locator. The passage distinguishes trade from ribā, addresses those who consume ribā, and culminates in 2:279 with a warning of war from Allah and His Messenger if the outstanding ribā claim is not abandoned.

For publication, the archive uses M. A. S. Abdel Haleem's 2004 English translation as its default English edition, while treating the Arabic Qur’anic text as the primary evidence. A verse locator is sufficient for the Arabic text; a print page is not required.

Text before interpretation

The central source-critical rule is simple: the Qur’anic wording should be distinguished from the later explanations built around it. The word ribā is the primary textual term. Later tafsir, jurisprudence and modern economic writing may explain its scope, but those explanations are additional layers of evidence.

The passage itself presents a contrast between trade and ribā, instructs believers to abandon outstanding ribā claims, and describes 2:279 in unusually forceful terms. The article therefore treats the “war” language as a feature of the text rather than importing a later explanation into the verse itself.

What the passage does not settle by itself

The verses are central to later Muslim legal and theological discussions, but the source record does not treat the Qur’an alone as a complete answer to every later question about financial instruments. In particular, the application of the category of ribā to modern bank interest is a later interpretive and jurisprudential question.

Scholarly discussions differ over the relationship between Qur’anic ribā and later legal categories. Those differences should be identified as interpretations rather than silently presented as if they were additional Qur’anic wording.

Why the language matters

The warning in 2:279 is historically significant precisely because it is unusually strong. A source-first reading does not need to dilute that language, nor does it need to assign a later legal meaning to it before examining the text.

The useful historical sequence is: establish the Qur’anic passage; identify its immediate textual claims; then examine tafsir, fiqh and modern interpretation as separate bodies of evidence. That sequence keeps the primary source visible while allowing later intellectual history to be studied on its own terms.
