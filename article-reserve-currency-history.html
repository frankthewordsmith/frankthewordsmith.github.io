A brief history of world reserve currency and their transition process

A source-conscious history of how international reserve currencies emerged, changed, overlapped and transitioned across different monetary systems.

1. What is a reserve currency?

A reserve currency is a currency held by monetary authorities as part of official foreign-exchange reserves. The term has not had one fixed meaning throughout history. Under the nineteenth-century classical gold standard, “reserve” referred primarily to the gold held to support convertibility. In the modern floating-currency system, official reserves are generally foreign-currency assets, often held to provide liquidity and a buffer against external financial pressures. The meaning of the term therefore changed as the international monetary system changed.

It is also useful to distinguish several related roles. A currency may be an international trade currency, a vehicle currency used in foreign-exchange transactions, an invoicing currency, a reserve currency, or an exchange-rate anchor. These roles can overlap without being identical. A currency can therefore become internationally important without immediately becoming the dominant reserve asset.

2. Before the modern reserve-currency system

Long before modern central banks, particular monies circulated beyond the political boundaries of their issuers. Historical accounts identify the Athenian silver drachma, Roman aureus, Byzantine solidus and Arabian dinar among currencies that acquired wide international use. Later, Mediterranean trade and the expansion of European commercial empires extended the reach of Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch and British monies.

These examples should not be treated as identical to a modern reserve currency. The institutional environment was different: central banks, modern foreign-exchange markets and official reserve reporting did not yet exist in their present forms. They nevertheless illustrate an enduring feature of monetary history: a currency can acquire international reach when it is supported by extensive trade, trusted settlement arrangements, political power and a financial infrastructure capable of handling cross-border claims.

3. The Dutch phase and the rise of financial settlement

The early modern Dutch Republic provides an important bridge between circulating international money and modern reserve assets. The Bank of Amsterdam, founded in 1609, created a highly trusted settlement institution whose bank guilder was backed by precious metals held in its vaults. The Bank’s ledger money allowed merchants to settle claims without relying entirely on the varying quality of circulating coin.

The significance of this development is institutional as much as monetary. International monetary leadership depends not only on the physical currency but also on the institutions that make claims denominated in that currency safe, liquid and transferable.

4. The classical gold standard and sterling

During the nineteenth century, the international monetary system became increasingly organized around gold convertibility. The transition from bimetallism toward the gold standard accelerated in the 1870s and 1880s. Major currencies were linked to gold at fixed rates, and international adjustment operated through the rules and constraints of that system.

Britain occupied a central position within this architecture. London was a leading financial centre, British trade and shipping connected markets across the world, and sterling was widely used for international transactions. Sterling therefore acquired a role that was larger than the domestic British economy alone: foreign governments, banks and merchants could hold sterling claims because they could use them in trade and financial markets centred on London.

Reserve-currency status in this period was closely connected to the credibility of convertibility, the depth of the issuing country’s financial market, the breadth of its commercial networks and the availability of safe assets denominated in the currency.

5. The First World War and the beginning of the sterling–dollar transition

The transition from sterling to the dollar was not a single event in 1944. Evidence from the interwar period places the dollar’s rise much earlier. The First World War disrupted European finance, strengthened the United States’ creditor position and expanded the international use of the dollar in trade and trade credit.

Research on the period 1914–1939 finds that the shift toward the dollar occurred during and immediately after the First World War, with policy support from the Federal Reserve contributing to the dollar’s international role. Other historical work using reserve-composition evidence places the dollar’s overtaking of sterling in the mid-1920s.

Sterling nevertheless remained important. Historical ties, the British Empire and the sterling area sustained its use, illustrating that reserve-currency transitions can involve long periods of coexistence rather than an immediate replacement.

6. The interwar period: coexistence, crisis and fragmentation

Between the two world wars, gold, sterling and the US dollar were major reserve assets. The attempt to restore the gold-exchange standard after the First World War faced serious strains. The return of sterling to gold in 1925 at the pre-war parity placed additional pressure on the British system, and Britain left gold permanently in September 1931.

The Great Depression further weakened the interwar monetary order. Exchange controls, competitive devaluations, bilateral payment arrangements and financial instability reduced the effectiveness of the earlier system. The result was not simply the disappearance of sterling; it was a fragmented monetary environment in which more than one major currency continued to perform international functions.

7. Bretton Woods: institutionalising the dollar

The Bretton Woods Conference of 1944 created a new international monetary framework. Major currencies were linked to the US dollar through fixed but adjustable exchange rates, while the dollar was fixed to gold at $35 per ounce. The International Monetary Fund was established to support the system.

This arrangement institutionalised the dollar’s central position. The dollar was both the principal reserve currency and the currency to which other major currencies were pegged. Foreign governments accumulated dollar claims, while US Treasury securities became important reserve assets.

The system did not mean that sterling immediately disappeared. Britain and other countries continued to hold and use sterling, and the pound remained important within the sterling area. But the post-war growth of US economic and financial capacity, together with the institutional structure of Bretton Woods, made the dollar the central currency of the new system.

8. The end of gold convertibility and the transition to floating currencies

On 15 August 1971, the United States suspended the dollar’s convertibility into gold for foreign official holders. The Bretton Woods fixed-exchange-rate framework subsequently broke down, and by 1973 the major currencies were floating against one another.

This was a fundamental transition in the monetary system, but it was not a transition away from the dollar as the leading reserve currency. The dollar remained central to international trade, payments, banking and financial markets. What changed was the monetary anchor: instead of a fixed dollar–gold relationship supporting the system, major currencies increasingly operated under domestic monetary-policy anchors and floating exchange rates.

9. The modern reserve-currency system

After Bretton Woods, reserve currencies increasingly became components of diversified official portfolios. The euro, yen, pound sterling, Swiss franc, Canadian dollar, Australian dollar and, later, renminbi all acquired identifiable roles in official reserves and international finance.

The euro’s creation in 1999 produced the most important new reserve currency of the late twentieth century. Its rise did not simply reproduce the earlier sterling-to-dollar transition. The modern system allows several currencies to perform reserve functions simultaneously, with the dollar retaining a large network advantage through trade, financial markets, banking, debt markets and established payment infrastructure.

The renminbi’s inclusion in the IMF’s Special Drawing Rights basket in 2016 was another institutional milestone. SDR inclusion recognised the renminbi as a freely usable currency within the IMF framework, but SDR status should not be confused with becoming the world’s dominant reserve currency.

10. How reserve-currency transitions actually happen

Historical transitions are better understood as overlapping processes than as a simple baton pass from one country to another. Several conditions tend to reinforce one another:

  • Economic scale: a large productive and trading economy generates a large volume of cross-border claims.
  • Financial-market depth: foreign holders need liquid markets in which to buy, sell and finance assets denominated in the currency.
  • Safe assets: governments and central banks need highly liquid assets for reserve management, especially government securities.
  • Convertibility and access: restrictions on moving capital or converting the currency can limit international reserve use.
  • Trade networks: exporters and importers have incentives to invoice and settle in currencies already widely used by their counterparties.
  • Banking infrastructure: international banks and payment networks reinforce currencies already embedded in cross-border finance.
  • Institutional credibility: reserve managers require confidence in monetary, fiscal and legal institutions.
  • Geopolitical and historical links: alliances, empires, regional blocs and long-established financial relationships can preserve an incumbent currency after its originating economy’s relative weight changes.
  • Network effects: a currency already widely used is attractive precisely because others already use it, making transitions gradual unless a major shock changes the incentives.

11. Why transitions can take decades

A reserve currency is embedded in contracts, bank balance sheets, government debt markets, trade invoices, foreign-exchange dealing and institutional habits. Replacing it therefore requires more than another country becoming economically larger.

The historical dollar–sterling transition demonstrates this inertia. The United States had become a major economic and financial power before the dollar fully displaced sterling across the international system. Sterling remained important after the dollar had begun to overtake it in particular functions.

For the same reason, a decline in a currency’s reserve share does not necessarily mean that another currency has already replaced it. A multi-currency system can persist for long periods, with different currencies performing different functions.

12. Reserve currencies and the foreign-exchange market

The foreign-exchange market provides another measure of international currency use. BIS data for April 2025 recorded average daily OTC foreign-exchange turnover of about $9.6 trillion. The US dollar was on one side of 89.2% of transactions, while the euro was on one side of 28.9%, the Japanese yen 16.8%, the pound sterling 10.2%, and the renminbi 8.5%.

These figures measure foreign-exchange market use, not reserve holdings. They therefore should not be substituted for reserve-share statistics. They do, however, show the scale of the dollar’s role as a vehicle currency and the continuing presence of several other major currencies.

13. Reserve holdings today

IMF COFER data provide the principal official dataset for the currency composition of global foreign-exchange reserves. In the IMF’s 2026 first-quarter data brief, total allocated foreign-exchange reserves were about $13.10 trillion. The US dollar accounted for 57.13%, the euro 20.03%, the Japanese yen 5.44% and the renminbi 1.99%. The remaining share was distributed among other currencies.

Reserve shares can move for several reasons besides deliberate diversification. Exchange-rate valuation changes, changes in bond prices and active reserve management can alter the measured composition. A fall in one currency’s percentage therefore does not by itself establish a complete transition to another currency.

14. A useful model of the transition process

The historical record suggests a recurring sequence, although no transition follows it mechanically:

  1. An economy expands its share of world production and trade.
  2. Its currency becomes more useful for international settlement.
  3. Financial institutions develop deeper markets in that currency.
  4. Foreign banks, firms and governments begin holding larger currency-denominated claims.
  5. The issuing state supplies liquid and trusted reserve assets.
  6. Central banks begin accumulating the currency as an official reserve asset.
  7. Network effects reinforce existing usage.
  8. A major political, financial or geopolitical shock can accelerate diversification.
  9. The incumbent and challenger may coexist for many years before their relative roles change materially.

This is a framework for historical comparison, not a prediction rule. The historical evidence shows that transitions can be delayed, interrupted or divided across different monetary functions.

16. The central historical lesson

The history of reserve currencies is not a simple list of countries taking turns. It is the history of monetary systems, financial markets, trade networks and institutions changing together.

The Dutch experience shows the importance of settlement institutions. The British experience shows how trade, finance and gold convertibility can reinforce one another. The sterling–dollar transition shows that international monetary leadership can overlap for decades. Bretton Woods shows how institutions can formalise an already developing currency hierarchy. The post-1971 system shows that a currency can remain dominant even after the monetary regime that originally anchored it has disappeared.

The modern system is therefore better described as a dollar-centred, multi-currency reserve and financial system than as a world in which only one currency performs every international monetary function.

17. Sources and evidentiary hierarchy

Institutional and primary-source foundations: IMF materials on the international monetary system, reserve currencies and COFER; Federal Reserve historical materials on Bretton Woods and the dollar; BIS historical materials on international currencies, Bretton Woods and foreign-exchange markets; Bank of England historical material on sterling and reserve currency functions.

Historical scholarship: Barry Eichengreen and Marc Flandreau on the rise of the dollar relative to sterling; IMF historical studies of reserve currencies and international monetary cooperation; BIS research on reserve composition and currency use.

Methodological rule: reserve-currency status should be distinguished from trade-currency, vehicle-currency and exchange-rate-anchor functions. Historical transitions should be described from dated evidence rather than inferred from a single modern reserve-share statistic.
