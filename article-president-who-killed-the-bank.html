The President Who Killed the Bank

An extensive source-conscious study of Andrew Jackson, the Second Bank of the United States, the Bank War, federal deposits, the Panic of 1837 and the eventual development of American central banking.

1. The institution Jackson set out to destroy

The Second Bank of the United States was created in 1816, after the War of 1812 exposed weaknesses in the fragmented American banking system. The First Bank's charter had expired in 1811. During the war, the federal government encountered serious financing difficulties while many state-chartered banks suspended specie payments. President James Madison signed the Second Bank's charter in April 1816, and the institution began operations in Philadelphia in January 1817.

The Bank had $35 million in capital, a twenty-year federal charter, a headquarters in Philadelphia, branches around the country, responsibility for holding federal deposits and an important role in federal payments. It also influenced the wider banking system by requiring state banks to redeem their notes in specie. It was not a modern central bank in the later Federal Reserve sense: it did not conduct modern monetary policy or operate with today's institutional framework.

2. Why the Bank was controversial

National banking was controversial before Andrew Jackson. Alexander Hamilton's First Bank had already generated constitutional disagreement. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall's Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the Bank and rejected Maryland's attempt to tax it. The decision relied heavily on the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Jackson nevertheless regarded the constitutional question as open to his own judgment when considering the Bank's recharter. The Bank War therefore became simultaneously a banking dispute, a constitutional dispute, a struggle over federal power and a political struggle over economic privilege.

3. The Bank's troubled beginning

The Second Bank did not operate perfectly until Jackson attacked it. Under its first president, William Jones, it expanded credit aggressively and then contracted it sharply. The institution's early management contributed to the financial instability surrounding the Panic of 1819.

Langdon Cheves succeeded Jones and tightened operations, reduced note circulation and demanded specie redemption from state banks. These measures strengthened the Bank's finances but also intensified contraction. The history matters because Jackson's later opposition developed against an institution that already had a controversial record.

4. Nicholas Biddle strengthens the Bank

Nicholas Biddle became president in 1823. Under Biddle, the Bank became a more effective national financial institution. It disciplined state-bank note issuance through specie redemption requirements, operated a large branch network and supplied a degree of financial integration across the country.

The paradox was that greater effectiveness also meant greater political power. The stronger the Bank became, the easier it was for opponents to portray it as an institution capable of exercising excessive influence over credit and public affairs.

5. Enter Andrew Jackson

Andrew Jackson became president in 1829 and already distrusted banks. His personal financial experiences contributed to his preference for specie, but his opposition was also political and constitutional. He believed the Bank concentrated excessive economic power, favored wealthy interests, possessed privileges unavailable to ordinary citizens and threatened state authority.

Jackson's first annual message to Congress in 1829 attacked the Bank even though its charter still had years to run. His opposition was therefore directed at the institution itself rather than being merely a reaction to an immediate banking failure.

6. Jackson's constitutional and economic argument

Jackson's July 10, 1832 veto message is the central primary source for understanding his position. He argued that the Bank possessed powers and privileges that were unconstitutional and dangerous. His objections included federal authority to create corporations, the Bank's reach into the states, concentration of wealth, foreign ownership and the scale of its capital.

These points should be presented as Jackson's documented arguments. The veto establishes what Jackson believed and argued; it does not by itself prove every economic or constitutional conclusion he drew from those premises.

7. The 1832 recharter battle

The Bank's charter did not expire until 1836, but Nicholas Biddle and his allies sought an early recharter in 1832. Senator Henry Clay, Jackson's presidential opponent, supported the measure. Congress passed the recharter bill, and Jackson vetoed it on July 10, 1832.

Jackson then won reelection in November. He interpreted the election as public support for his opposition to the Bank. The veto did not itself immediately close the Bank: its existing federal charter remained valid until 1836.

8. Removing the federal deposits

Jackson's next step was to remove federal deposits from the Second Bank and place them in selected state-chartered banks, later called the “pet banks.” Treasury Secretary William J. Duane resisted the policy and was removed. On September 23, 1833, Attorney General Roger B. Taney, serving as acting Treasury secretary, carried out the removal.

This transformed the Bank War. Jackson was no longer merely opposing recharter; his administration was actively weakening the institution's financial position by withdrawing federal government balances.

9. Why the deposits mattered

Federal deposits were economically significant. Their withdrawal reduced the Bank's deposits and therefore its financial influence. The government's funds were redistributed among state-chartered institutions, helping decentralize American banking.

But decentralization created a different institutional environment. Instead of one powerful national institution, the country increasingly depended upon numerous state banks operating under differing state rules.

10. Biddle strikes back

Nicholas Biddle responded by restricting credit and calling in loans. His strategy was intended to create financial pressure and demonstrate the Bank's importance, potentially forcing the administration to restore the deposits or reconsider recharter.

The strategy failed politically. The credit contraction instead reinforced the argument that the Bank possessed too much power. This is one of the central ironies of the Bank War: Biddle's attempt to use financial power against Jackson helped demonstrate why Jackson's opponents feared concentrated banking power.

11. The Senate censures Jackson

Jackson's actions generated a constitutional confrontation. On March 28, 1834, the Senate formally censured him, stating that he had assumed powers not granted by the Constitution and laws. The dispute centred particularly on the removal of federal deposits and the dismissal of Treasury Secretary Duane.

Jackson rejected the Senate's judgment. In 1837, after his political allies had regained control of the Senate, the censure resolution was formally expunged from the Senate journal.

12. Did Jackson actually kill the Bank?

In the institutional sense, Jackson killed the Second Bank as a federally chartered national institution. He vetoed its recharter, won reelection while making the Bank a major issue, removed federal deposits and prevented restoration of its federal charter.

But he did not simply close the Bank in 1833. Its federal charter expired in 1836. Pennsylvania subsequently granted the institution a state charter, and it continued as the United States Bank of Pennsylvania until its eventual failure in the early 1840s.

The most precise formulation is therefore: Jackson destroyed the Second Bank as a federally chartered national institution.

13. What replaced it?

The United States did not immediately create another national central bank. For decades, American banking became more decentralized. State-chartered banks proliferated, and the federal government lacked a modern central bank capable of acting as lender of last resort or conducting national monetary policy.

Repeated financial crises eventually demonstrated the limitations of this arrangement. The Panic of 1907 helped create the political momentum for the Federal Reserve System in 1913. The long sequence is therefore: First Bank, Second Bank, Bank War, decentralized banking, repeated crises and eventually the Federal Reserve.

14. Did Jackson cause the Panic of 1837?

The simple chain “Jackson killed the Bank, therefore the Panic of 1837 occurred” is not sufficient. Historians debate the relative importance of Jacksonian policy, the redistribution of government balances, specie flows, land speculation, cotton markets, banking conditions and international financial developments.

Research on Jacksonian monetary policy emphasizes the movement of government balances and the resulting demand for specie in western states. Other research emphasizes the tightening of credit in Britain and the interconnected international financial crisis. A source-first treatment should therefore distinguish documented policy effects from later claims about sole causation.

15. The Specie Circular

Jackson's Specie Circular of 1836 required payment in gold or silver for federal public-land purchases. It reflected his preference for hard money and his opposition to speculative expansion based on bank credit.

Its relationship to the Panic of 1837 should not be reduced to a single-cause explanation. Historical studies indicate that international financial developments and credit conditions were also important.

16. The deeper monetary philosophy

At the centre of Jackson's opposition was a disagreement about money and political power. Jackson's preferred monetary world emphasized gold and silver, limited reliance on bank credit, suspicion of concentrated financial power and greater decentralization.

The Bank represented the opposing tendency: centralized financial authority, large-scale credit, banknotes, national financial integration and an institution capable of disciplining state banks. The Bank therefore became a symbol of competing visions of American political economy.

17. Was the Bank simply a private monopoly?

Jackson described the Bank as a privileged institution, but its legal structure was more complicated. The federal government held shares, five of the twenty-five directors were appointed by the president with Senate confirmation, and the Bank served as the federal government's fiscal agent. At the same time, it was a commercial corporation whose private shareholders expected returns.

Its dual character—public responsibilities combined with private ownership—was central to the political controversy.

18. Foreign ownership

Jackson's veto message paid particular attention to foreign ownership of Bank stock. He argued that foreign investors could acquire influence over an institution affecting the American economy. The primary source establishes Jackson's argument; it should not automatically be treated as proof that foreign investors actually controlled American monetary policy.

19. The political transformation

The Bank War helped reshape the American party system. Jackson's supporters increasingly identified with the Democratic Party, while opponents who resisted his policies organized around the Whig Party. The conflict therefore became a struggle not only over banking but also over presidential authority and the distribution of economic power.

20. The constitutional dimension

The Supreme Court had already ruled in McCulloch v. Maryland that the Bank was constitutional. Jackson nevertheless vetoed its recharter. His position asserted that the president retained an independent constitutional responsibility to judge legislation presented to him.

The Bank War therefore belongs in the history of American constitutional government as well as in the history of banking.

21. Jackson's victory was not the end of central banking

Jackson succeeded in destroying the Second Bank's federal charter, but the underlying economic problems did not disappear. The United States still needed a stable currency, banking supervision, financial liquidity, management of federal funds and mechanisms for responding to systemic crises.

The nineteenth century therefore became a long experiment in decentralized banking. The creation of the Federal Reserve in 1913 represented a later attempt to solve problems that the Bank War had not permanently eliminated.

22. The central historical irony

Jackson believed concentrated banking power threatened democratic government. Destroying the Second Bank did not eliminate concentrated financial power; it changed the institutional structure through which financial power operated.

The post-Bank-War system produced a much more decentralized collection of state-chartered banks. That arrangement altered access to banking and the allocation of credit, while also leaving the country without a national central-bank mechanism during later crises.

23. Modern historical interpretation

There is no single scholarly interpretation of the Bank War. One tradition emphasizes Jackson's opposition to concentrated economic privilege. Another emphasizes the Second Bank's contribution to monetary and financial stability. Other approaches focus on constitutional conflict, presidential power, credit allocation, banking competition and the causes of the Panic of 1837.

Modern economic research likewise examines both short-run disruption and longer-run effects of the destruction of the Second Bank. These interpretations should remain distinct from the primary documentary record.

25. Primary sources and evidentiary hierarchy

Primary evidence: Jackson's July 10, 1832 Bank veto; Jackson's September 18, 1833 message concerning removal of the public deposits; McCulloch v. Maryland (1819); congressional debates; Senate censure proceedings; Treasury documents; Second Bank records; and contemporary correspondence and newspapers.

Institutional historical research: Federal Reserve historical materials, Library of Congress collections, U.S. Senate historical records and FRASER archival material.

Academic economic history: research on Jacksonian monetary policy, the Second Bank, banking decentralization and the Panic of 1837, including work by Peter L. Rousseau and other economic historians.

Methodological rule: keep Jackson's stated arguments separate from later historical interpretation, distinguish correlation from causation, and do not attribute the Panic of 1837 to one cause without addressing competing explanations.

26. The central research question

The documentary record makes Jackson's role in destroying the Second Bank's federal charter clear. The deeper question is what happened to American money, credit, banking power and presidential authority when he destroyed it.

That question opens a broader investigation into why Jackson distrusted banking, how effective Biddle's institution actually was, whether Jackson's constitutional argument was compatible with McCulloch, what happened after federal deposits were removed, how the Panic of 1837 developed, and why the United States eventually recreated a central-banking system in 1913.

27. The historical chain

First Bank fails to receive recharter (1811) → War of 1812 exposes weaknesses of decentralized banking → Second Bank established (1816) → early management problems and Panic of 1819 → Biddle strengthens the Bank → Jackson attacks Bank → 1832 recharter veto → federal deposits removed (1833) → Biddle contracts credit → Senate censure (1834) → federal charter expires (1836) → former Bank continues under Pennsylvania charter → Panic of 1837 → decades without a national central bank → Panic of 1907 → Federal Reserve created (1913).
